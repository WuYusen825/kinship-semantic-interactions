unthinkable categories and the
fundamental laws of kinship


PER HAGE— University of Utah




  One eventually begins to wonder whether Nature herself does not abhor disjunctive groupings.

                                          —J.S. Bruner, J.J. Goodnow, and G.A. Austin, A Study of Thinking

   In L'Exercice de la parente, Franchise Heritier (1981) develops a general theory of kinship
systems based on two "fundamental laws" (lois fondamentales). These laws depend for their
proof on a sociological explanation of two "unthinkable" (impensables)—that is, theoretically
possible but empirically unrealized kinship terminologies implicit in the typologies of Lowie
(1968(1929]) and Murdock (1949). Terray (1986) rejects this explanation on logical grounds
but is unable to give any reason for the nonoccurrence of these two kinship terminologies. There
is, however, a cognitive-linguistic explanation. According to Greenberg (1966, 1990(1980]),
kinship terminologies, such as the unrealized one in Lowie's typology, do not occur, or are rare,
because of a universal tendency to avoid disjunctive categories. With the notable exception of
Nerloveand Romney (1967) and subsequently Kronenfeld (1974), Greenberg's theory has been
overlooked by most anthropologists and is not mentioned in research works or discourses on
kinship analysis (e.g., Barnard and Good 1984; Needham 1971).1 This is unfortunate because,
as Kroeber (1909) insisted and as Lowie (1968(1929]) conceded, there are certain aspects of
kinship terminologies that can only be explained in cognitive-linguistic terms.
   I have a double aim. The first is to evaluate He>itier's laws in the light of Greenberg's theory
of universals in kinship classification. The second is to point out two important but unnoticed
cognitive-linguistic features of Lowie's classification: his implicit lattice model of terminological
change and his recognition that kinship terminologies may become simpler rather than more
complex in the course of time. The lattice, or, more generally, semi lattice model, first introduced
by Epling, Kirk, and Boyd (1973) in the analysis of Polynesian sibling terms, and later applied
by Hage and Harary (1996) to Oceanic sibling terms, shows how conjunctively defined kinship
terminologies evolve through successive binary partition—in other words, through the succes-
sive addition or deletion of binary features. It captures multilinear as well as unilinear, and
converging as well as diverging, paths in the evolution of all kinship terminologies. The model
was in fact anticipated by Lowie in his classification of first ascending-generation terminologies.2


In L'Exercice de la parente, Heritier develops a general theory of kinship systems
based on two "fundamental laws." These laws depend for their proof on a
sociological explanation of two logically possible but empirically unrealized
terminologies in the classifications of Lowie and Murdock. Following Greenberg's
cognitive-linguistic theory of kinship classification, I interpret these unrealized
terminologies in the first instance as special cases of a universal tendency to avoid
disjunctive categories. In developing this interpretation I introduce an evolutionary
perspective that has been absent from most discussions of kinship theory fora long
time. The structural models for this analysis are, as always, graph theoretic, [kinship
theory, cognition, linguistics, graph theory]

                                                                                  Heritier's theory

   Heritier's book constitutes, as Terray (1986) says, the most decisive progress in the analysis
of kinship systems since the publication of Levi-Strauss's Les Structures elementaires de la
parente (1949). In intricately related arguments it explains the general laws underlying kinship
terminologies, reveals the hidden correspondences between terminologies, descent modes, and
marriage rules, and elucidates the functioning of semicomplex marriage systems and, by
generalization, that of complex systems as well. Our concern is with the laws that underlie
kinship terminologies.
   The axiomatic basis of Heritier's theory consists of three biological facts: the duality of the
sexes, the succession of generations, and differences within a generation due to birth order.
Heritier's "first fundamental law of kinship" holds that "cross-solidarity is never stronger than
parallel solidarity and a cross-relation between individuals or groups is never the implicit basis
of equivalence or identity" (Heritier 1981:38, my translation). This law is expressed in the
classification of relatives in the first ascending generation. According to Lowie there are four
types of terminologies:4

1. Generational: F = FB = MB.
2. Lineal: F * FB = MB.
3. Bifurcate merging: F = FB * MB.
4. Bifurcate collateral: F * FB * MB.

There is, however, as Heritier points out, a fifth logically possible but empirically unrealized
type not considered by Lowie:

5. F = M B * F B .

According to Heritier this type does not occur because it is "unthinkable that the relation
between two men linked through a woman, sister of one, wife of the other, could be closer than
the relation between two brothers" (1981:42). She observes, however, that one could imagine
a society in which this fifth possibility was realized. Under a rule of patrilineal descent and
amitalocal residence, a rule that, as Murdock (1949) noted, is not found in any society, wives
would take their Hs to reside with the brides' FZs. The head of the resulting amitaclan, the FZ,
would be succeeded by her BD. The ideal marriage would be with FBD: this would permit a
man to reside with his amitaclan and provide male assistance to the clan doyenne. Under this
rule F could be classed with MB as "mother's kin," that is, as an affine or consanguine of M and
distinguished from FB. Such an arrangement is "unthinkable" in that it "gives more weight,
residential stability and superiority to the feminine principle than to the masculine principle. If
inversely, a matrilineal avunculocal system is thinkable it is because it gives more weight,
residential stability and superiority to the masculine principle" (1981:51). HeYitier concludes
from this thought experiment that the second fundamental law of kinship is male dominance
and that this law is the "condition of the first."5
   Terray (1986) objects that Heritier's thought experiment, although plausible, assumes a
biconditional relation (une correspondance bi-univoque) between kinship terminology and
social practice and therefore cannot be regarded as "proof" of her proposed laws of kinship:6
  The problem posed by such a simulation is that of the relation between terminology and social practice.

   If social practice cannot account for the nonoccurrence of the fifth logically possible type of
kinship terminology implicit in Lowie's classification, then what can? Barnard and Good (1964)
call attention to this type but are unable to give any explanation for its nonoccurrence. In the
symmetrical case of female terminologies in the first ascending generation, Murdock (1949)
noted the rarity of the fifth type (M = FZ * MZ) but otherwise offered no comment.7 The answer
was given many years ago by Greenberg (1966,1990[1980]).


Greenberg's theory

  In Greenberg'sfirstpaper on kinship classification (1966) he observed that the fifth logically
possible terminology implicit in Lowie's typology does not occur because it groups together the
two most divergent relatives, F and MB, without including FB. In his second paper, Greenberg
(1990(1980]) analyzed the symmetrical case of female relatives in the parents' generation and
specified that the fifth type (M = FZ * MZ) does not occur because it contains a "disjunctive*
category:
  The principle involved is the avoidance of logically disjunctive definitions. There is no way of demarcating
  by a single set of defining properties a term which embraces the mother and the Ifather's] sister without
  including in its reference the [mother's] sister. This is because mother's sister shares matrilineality with
  mother and collaterality with father's sister, but there is no common property oi mother and father's sister,
  the two most different terms, except female, first ascending generation, and this includes mother's sister
  in its reference. [Greenberg 1990(1980):320]

In Bruner, Goodnow, and Austin's seminal work in cognitive psychology (1956) a conjunctive
category is "one defined by the joint presence of the appropriate value of several attributes"
(1956:41). A disjunctive category is one defined by the presence of one or another of these
attributes.8 In concept-attainment experiments in which a subject is required to learn categories
defined by the attributes of figure (squares, circles, or crosses), color (red, green, or black), and
number (one, two, or three figures), an example of a conjunctive category is one defined by
three red circles. A disjunctive category is one that has "three red circles or any constituent
thereof: three figures, red figures, circles, three red figures, red circles or three circles" (Bruner
et al. 1956:41). Experiments show that disjunctive categories are much more difficult to learn
than conjunctive ones. The reasons are that "one can predict only faultily or probabilistically
from class membership to the properties of members [and that] two positive exemplars of a class
may share no common characteristic" (Bruner et al. 1956:180). In addition, in learning a
disjunctive category, one must rely on negative instances; in other words, one must determine
what is not a member of a category.
   Generalizing from these results in psychology to kinship terminologies in anthropology,
Nerlove and Romney (1967) proposed that sibling terminologies be viewed "as an outcome of
a 'natural' cognitive experiment." They predicted that disjunctive sibling terminologies would
have "low survival value" and would therefore be extremely rare. Their hypothesis was
dramatically confirmed: in a worldwide sample oi 240 sibling terminologies only 4 were found
to contain disjunctive categories.

It is convenient to represent the semantic structure of kinship terminologies using the graph
theoretic model of a twin binary tree (Hage and Harary 1996). The model is more revealing
than lists as in Greenberg (1966) and more immediate than spatial representations as in
D'Andrade (1995). A conjunctive kinship category is one that can be represented by a single
endnode of a twin binary tree while a disjunctive category is one that cannot. Greenberg defines
the kinship terminologies in Lowie's typology by the dimensions (attributes) of (1) collaterally:
lineal (ancestral) versus collateral (nonancestral)—I versus c; and (2) side: patrilineal (father's
side) versus matrilineal (mother's side)—p versus m. Figure 1a shows twin binary tree models
of the four types distinguished by Lowie plus the fifth logically possible but empirically
unrealized type noted by Greenberg. The letters A, B, C represent native kinship terms
(categories). The first four types have only conjunctive categories while the fifth type has a
disjunctive category (or is disjunctive for short).
   H£ritier defines these terminologies slightly differently (Figure 1b). Instead of side she uses
the dimension of parity: parallel (I I) relatives are parents and parents' same sex siblings while
cross (x) relatives are parents' opposite sex siblings. As in Greenberg's analysis only the fifth
"unthinkable" terminology is disjunctive.10 Notice that in this case it does not make any
difference which dimensions—parity or side—we use.

marking rules All four of the logically possible, conjunctive first ascending-generation
kinship terminologies in Lowie's typology are empirically realized, but this is not true in general.
In Greenberg's theory there are three determinants of realizable kinship terminologies: the
avoidance of disjunctive categories, salience, and marking. Salience refers to a hierarchical
relationship between different dimensions of contrast. For example, sex of relative is more salient
than sex of connecting relative "in that systems which neutralize the former but not the latter
are infrequent whereas those which neutralize the latter but not the former are extremely
common" (Greenberg 1990[1980]:321).
   Marking refers to a hierarchical relationship between values of the same dimension. The
unmarked term of an opposition is more general than the marked term. Marking applies to all
languages and to every level of a language—phonological, grammatical, and lexical. In English
and other European languages, for example, the present tense is unmarked as against the past,
and the term man is unmarked as against the term woman}2 In kinship, according to Greenberg,
the marked term has three characteristics:13

1. Overt expression. For example, in English kinship terminology the term "grandparent" is
   marked while "parent" is unmarked.
2. Fewer distinctions. In English the sex distinction is neutralized in the marked category
   "cousin" as opposed to the categories "brother" and "sister."
3. Defectivation: the marked term lacks an intersecting category. In English there are terms for
   "brother-in-law" and "sister-in-law" but not for "cousin-in-law."

Summarizing a wealth of cross-cultural evidence, Greenberg (1990[1980]:318) proposes that
"lineal is unmarked as against collateral, consanguineal is unmarked as against affinal, male is
unmarked as against female in regard to sex of referent, older is unmarked in relation to younger.... In
general, the closer a generation is to ego, the more unmarked it is/7 We say "proposes" because,
as Greenberg (1987b:369) emphasized in reply to a criticism by Scheffler (1987), the validity
of these criteria "has not been taken for granted, since every application is in effect also a test."
One such test was made by Nerlove and Romney (1967), who used marking hypotheses derived
from Greenberg (together with constraints of cognitive economy) to reduce the logically possible
146 conjunctive sibling terminologies to the 12 that occur with significant frequency.

terminologies. For example, "Crow and Omaha systems generally, perhaps always, require
matrilineal and patrilineal descent respectively" (1990[1980]:324). But even here linguistic

evolutionary semilattices In his paper on kinship terminology Lowie did more than merely
distinguish among four different types. He related each type to worldwide geographical areas
and he also considered possible evolutionary changes in kinship terminologies, conceiving of
such changes in binary terms and as proceeding from complex to simple rather than conversely
(as often happens with linguistic structures). Thus Lowie observed:

1. "The [bifurcate merging] system, which Morgan and Rivers genetically connect with the
   [generation] system (though in a reverse sense) is not one whit further from the [bifurcate
   collateral system]" (1968[1929]:42).
2. In Asian systems, "it is evident that emphasis on status and seniority would cause a drift from
   the bifurcate merging toward the generation pattern" (1968[1929]:49).
3. "Morgan was not warranted in assuming that the Polynesian term translated [as] 'father'
   implied procreation: the facts simply reveal a common status term for all kindred of one
   generation. Simplicity here, as in many other linguistic phenomena, is a late development,
   not a badge of antiquity. Three independent investigators, Sternberg in Siberia, Rivers in
   Oceania, Lowie in North America, have been able to show recent transformations of
   bifurcate merging nomenclatures in the direction of generation systems" (1968[1929]:56).
4. "The designations here employed are awkward but serve to bring out connections usually
   disregarded. Specifically, Morgan and Rivers stressed the genetic relationship of Generation
   and Bifurcate Merging terminologies: Morgan traced the development of the latter from the
   former; while Rivers reversed the process. The recognition of Bifurcate Collateral terminolo-
   gies opens a new prospect—the derivation of the Bifurcate Merging from the Bifurcate
   Collateral type. Logically, the affiliation is not one iota smaller than between the two systems
   compared by Morgan and Rivers. . . . When, for some reason—say the joint presence of the
   levirate and the sororate—partial merging develops, the Bifurcate Merging type would come
   into being" (1928:266-267).

The evolutionary changes described by Lowie have the structure of a "lattice" (Hage and Harary
1996). In a digraph (directed graph) D (Harary et al. 1965), an upper bound w of two nodes u
and v in D is a node that can reach both u and v by directed paths. Further, w is a least upper
bound (LUB) for u and v if it is not only an upper bound, but, for any other upper bound w',
every path from w ' t o either u or vmust contain w. The directional dual of the LUB (u, v) is their
greatest lower bound (GLB), defined similarly.
   A lattice is a partially ordered set in which every two nodes have both an LUB and a CLB. A
semilattice is a partially ordered set in which every two nodes have an LUB. It follows that every
lattice is a semilattice but not vice versa. In this sense a semilattice is a more general
mathematical structure than a lattice.
   It is customary to represent a lattice (or a semilattice) by its "Hasse diagram." In a digraph D
node u covers node w if there is no node v (uvw). In the Hasse diagram of a lattice only the arcs
of its covering digraph are drawn. It is also a convention to omit the directional arrows but I
will retain them for clarity.14

                                                                                                conclusion


   Following Greenberg's theory we conclude that the unthinkable kinship terminologies in
Lowie's and Murdock's typologies should be viewed in the first instance as special cases of a
cognitive-linguistic tendency to avoid disjunctive categories. This tendency is found in all
kinship terminologies, including those not based on the cross-parallel distinction. It is so
widespread in classification systems generally that one could speak of the mind's "abhorrence
of disjunctiveness" (Bruner et al. 1956). In cognitive anthropology the avoidance of disjunctive
categories was the original motivation and the basic assumption of the formal analysis of kinship
terminologies. As Lounsbury said in his pioneering paper on the subject,

  A componential definition represents a Boolean class product, and is thus a "unitary" or "conjunctive"
  definition. It is assumed that the meaning of any term belonging to a properly defined paradigm—one
  whose semantic field is itself unitary—will be susceptible to such a definition. This is perhaps a stronger
  item of faith than we have a right to hold at this moment; but it furnishes the motivation for the analysis
  of kinship systems at least.... Were we to compromise on this point and admit disjunctive definitions
  (class sums, alternative criteria for membership) as on a par with conjunctive definitions (class products,
  uniform criteria for membership), there would be no motivation for analysis in the first place, for definitions
  of kin classes by the summing of discrete members . . . are disjunctive definitions par excellence.
  [1968(1964):127]

In kinship analysis it may not be possible to avoid some disjunctive categories, but that is always
the aim. That is why Goodenough (1980), for example, prefers his own analysis of Kariera
terminology to that of Romney and Epling (1958) and Reid (1967). In everyday life, disjunctive
categories are avoided when possible, sometimes by being made to appear conjunctive.
Kronenfeld (n.d.) gives the example of treating siblings as lineal ("direct") relatives "even though
they are not directly on ego's ancestor/descendant line by conceiving of them as on the
ancestor/descendant line of ego's nuclear family and not noticing that ego's ancestors and
descendants, respectively, pertain to different nuclear families—one of orientation and
procreation."
  In scientific research, as Bruner, Goodnow, and Austin observe, the discovery of a disjunctive
explanation "seems to act as a challenge to future research rather than as an occasion to
celebrate a 'finding' " (1956:162). In cross-cultural studies, for example, such a challenge is
evident in attempts to explain bifurcate merging kinship terminology. In Murdock's reading
(1947), Rivers (1914) explained bifurcate merging terminology by the presence of moieties in
a society. Lowie (1915, 1917, 1919) "supplemented" this explanation by showing its relation
to exogamy. Finally, Kroeber (1917) attributed bifurcate merging terminology to the presence
of unilineal descent groups. In effect, Kroeber gave a more general, conjunctive explanation
that "subsumes the essential factors dealt with by Rivers and Lowie" (Murdock 1947:62).
  The subject of this paper goes back to a classic argument in anthropology as to whether
kinship terms are primarily determined by linguistic and psychological factors, as Kroeber (1909)
held, or by sociological conditions, as Rivers (1914) maintained. In attempting to strike a balance
between these positions, Lowie concluded that "there will always be residual phenomena
resisting interpretation on any but linguistic lines. This means that they are in a sense unique
facts that can be understood after they have been observed but that could not be deduced from
general principles" (1968[1929] :59). It is clear from Greenberg's theory that cognitive-linguistic
