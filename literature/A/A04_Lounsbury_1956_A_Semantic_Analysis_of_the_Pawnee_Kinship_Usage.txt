                                       I. INTRODUCTION

   1. LINGUISTICS, SEMANTICS, AND PRAGMATICS. The subject of this paper is
not in strictest terms a linguistic one. 'Linguistics', in the narrow sense in which
this term is currently used, is understood to concern itself solely with the for-
mal properties of languages. That is to say, it is limited to the analysis of the
properties of the signal systems or 'codes' themselves as inferred from the struc-
ture of messages, while it excludes on the one hand the primary nonlinguistic
stimuli which prompt messages or are encoded in them, and on the other hand
also the nonlinguistic responses which the messages may evoke. One need only
remind oneself of Bloomfield's description of 'speaker's stimuli' and 'hearer's
responses' for an illustration of the sorts of things that get excluded from lin-
guistics.' These things which get excluded from linguistics proper, however, are
to a large extent the concern and the subject matter of cultural anthropology,
and they belong within the fuller study of language.
   This division of the total context surrounding a linguistic event has been
recognized by a number of writers. A well-known formulation is that of Charles
W. Morris. For any sign system, linguistic or other, there are distinguished:
(a) the properties of the signs and their systematic relations to each other, or
'syntactics'; (b) the relations of the signs to their areas of designation and their
features of signification,2 or 'semantics'; and (c) the relations of these to be-
havior in response to signs, or 'pragmatics'.3 This scheme, applied to the study
of language, would isolate the physical and the relational properties of linguistic
signs as the subject matter of a special branch of study, while problems of mean-
ing and of the social functions of language would constitute two additional
fields of investigation. Precisely this departmentalization has been taking place
in the last twenty-five years, and the first of these branches has crystallized as
the science of 'linguistics'. The other two potential branches of the study of
language have not been as systematically developed and have not been disen-
tangled from each other in as clear-cut a manner as linguistics has been disen-
tangled from them. They are dealt with in various manners according to the
several tastes and interests of philosophers, lexicographers, philologists, general
semanticists, anthropologists, and psychologists. They present problems
partly of a different kind from those presented by linguistics, and partly of a
similar kind though more complex.

                         II. THE PAWNEE KINSHIP SYSTEM

  4. DATA. Given below is a portion of the kinship vocabulary of the Republi-
can Pawnee as we derive it from the data published by Lewis Henry Morgan.8
For purposes of this paper we include only the consanguineal and the spouse
terms. Definitions of meanings are given by listing as many kin types as are
known to be included in each kin class.9 By 'known' we mean either given di-
rectly by Morgan or inferable from reciprocal types or from sibling types given
by him. In a very few instances rules of succession, when these are unambigu-
ous, have been utilized also to fill in certain data. All types which are inferred
in one of these ways are marked with an asterisk. Those without the asterisk are
in Morgan's tables. As definitions, these listings are not exhaustive, for Mor-
gan's schedule was not as complete as we should desire; yet they will suffice
for an analysis.
  Primary kin-types are abbreviated as follows: F = 'father', M = 'mother',
B = 'brother', S = 'sister', s = 'son', d = 'daughter', H = 'husband', W =
'wife'. Secondary and higher-degree kin-types'o are abbreviated with compound
symbols: BW = 'brother's wife', MBd = 'mother's brother's daughter', FSs =
'father's sister's son', etc. All kin-types listed are assumed to be possible refer-
ents of the given kinship term for Egos of either sex, unless (a) a specific quali-
fier is added, such as 'FOR A MALE EGO', or (b) the kin-type is inherently re-
stricted to Egos of only one sex, such as H and HM, which are applicable only
by or in relation to a female.
   Translation labels are also given for the Pawnee terms. These, of course, are
not accurate either as definitions or as translations. They serve only to give us
convenient English tags for the forms and their referents. In each case the
translation label is the word which we would use in English, in our kinship
usage, to refer to the nearest one of the kin-types in each Pawnee class. The
extensions of each of these terms to additional kin-types, however, is very dif-
ferent in Pawnee from what it is in English. English terms when used as labels
for Pawnee classes are written with double quotation marks, thus distinguishing
them from the same words when used in their ordinary sense as labels for Eng-
lish classes. Thus "uncle" is not the same as 'uncle'.

  5. DESIGNATUM, DENOTATUM, AND SIGNIFICATUM. There are two ways of
defining a class: by naming the members of the class, or by stating the defining
features of the class, that is to say, the necessary and sufficient conditions for
membership in the class. These two ways are well known to us from phonemics.
When we define a given phoneme by listing the allophonic types which belong
to it, we are defining it by naming its members. When we define it as the class
of phones sharing such-and-such distinctive phonetic features, we are defining
it in the second way. In the case of our Pawnee kin classes we have begun with
definitions of the first variety. These reveal one aspect of the structure of the
system, namely the specific segmentation of the semantic field. They do not
show, however, the underlying principles of organization. These will be shown
only if we can proceed from the definitions-by-naming in such a way as to ar-
rive at definitions in terms of distinctive semantic features, and further, if we
can formulate the semantic structure of the whole set.

  The Pawnee tribe during the historical period consisted of four bands: the
Skiri, the Pitahawirat, the Kitkahaxki, and the Tsawi. Morgan's 'Republican
Pawnee', located then on the Republican River near the present boundary
between Kansas and Nebraska, were Kitkahaxki, His 'Grand Pawnee' were
Tsawi. The 'tribe' was not a political unit, but a linguistic and cultural one,
resulting from common origins.
   The bands were political units, each being a loose federation of independent
villages. The chiefs of the villages formed the band council. The number of
villages in a band probably ranged approximately from ten to fifteen. The Skiri
were reported at one time to have fifteen villages, but later were reduced to
thirteen. The other bands may have been comparable or somewhat smaller.
The band, although it was a political unit, can hardly be considered a true social
unit. Social life was confined to a smaller unit, the village.
  Villages were for the most part independent of each other, except for their
rather loose political affiliations into bands. The village was the society; it was
the unit within which most social interaction took place. It also constituted the
breeding group, being essentially endogamous. Marriages outside the village
were rare. The average size of the villages can be only roughly estimated. Village
populations probably ranged from one hundred to perhaps three hundred persons.
The total population of the Pawnee in the early 19th century has been variously
estimated at 4000 to 12,000.
  Every village had its sacred bundle, which was its supernatural charter and a
focus of religious rites. Each village had a priest and a chief, the rulers respec-
tively over sacred and secular matters. These statuses were passed on in the
male line. Class distinctions were marked, in spite of the small scale of these
village societies. The basic dichotomy was between nobility and commoners.
The nobility consisted for the most part of the families and agnatic kinsmen of
priests, chiefs, and medicine men, together with warriors and those wealthy in
horses, material possessions, and prestige. Nobility was for the most part a
supernatural attribute, hereditary in the male line. Within the warrior group,
however, where status could be achieved, there was apparently some room for
upward mobility. The noble class seems to have accounted for a little over half
of the population in many villages. Commoners were those poor in worldly
