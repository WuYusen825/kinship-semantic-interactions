        sibling typology: beyond Nerlove and Romney'



                             DAVID B. KRONENFE LD-University of California, Riverside




   In their paper, "Sibling Typology and Cross-Sex Behavior," Nerlove and Romney
(1967) introduce a typology of sibling terminologies. They use a number of assumptions
based on considerations of cognitive economy and on Greenberg's work on kinship
terminological universals to predict which subset of the logically possible types will occur
empirically. Their systematic consideration of the full logically possible set of
terminological categorizations of siblings and their use of an explicit set of assumptions
about the criteria that logically possible terminological categorizations must meet if they
are to occur empirically represent impressive contributions to the anthropological study
of semantic systems. Equally important is their attempt to derive their assumptions-
about the criteria that types must meet in order to exist empirically-from general
cognitive and linguistic principles that come from work done on topics other than sibling
terms and, where possible, on topics outside of kinship.
   However, their specific set of assumptions are not, in fact, sufficient to completely
reduce the set of logically possible types to the set of empirically occurrent types, i.e., to
eliminate the subset of logically possible types that cannot occur empirically and leave
only the subset of logically possible types that do in fact occur empirically. In order to
describe my improvement of their analysis I will first briefly characterize their argument
and the assumptions they use in it. Next, I will demonstrate which logically possible types
are not excluded by their assumptions (even though Nerlove and Romney intend that
these types should be excluded). I will complete the major part of the paper by offering
improved versions of their assumptions that do in fact exclude these types. My improved
versions of their assumptions will be stated in a form that is both more concise than theirs
and has a more general range of application than theirs does. In some cases, my versions
of their assumptions will be derived more directly and/or more plausibly from the
cognitive and linguistic principles or findings that they cite.
   These corrections and restatements are important because of the originality and
scientific value of Nerlove and Romney's form of analysis. Their deduction of attributes
of linguistic categories from experimental findings in cognitive psychology is particularly
significant for anthropology because of the many claims that have been made about the

            I will conclude the paper with a brief discussion of an empirical case in which one
         might have expected that the Nerlove and Romney assumptions would not hold, but in
         which they did in fact hold. This discussion will suggest one hypothesis to explain the
         fact that Nerlove and Romney found only a few cases that apparently did not meet their
         assumptions.


         Nerlove and Romney's analysis


            Nerlove and Romney's analysis begins with the fact that true sibling relations are
         composed of three variables: sex of speaker, sex of relative, and relative age. Each of
         these variables is independent of the others, and each has two possible values. These three
         binary variables produce eight (i.e., 23) possible kintypes: mome, momy, fome, fomy,
         mofe, mofy, fofe, fofy (f is a female person; m is a male person; o is a sibling link
         between two persons; e signifies that the relative is elder than the speaker; y signifies that
         the relative is younger than the speaker). Nerlove and Romney compute that there exist
         4,140 logically possible categorizations of these eight kintypes (cf. Table 1). A
         categorization is a set of categories (i.e., kinterms) such that each kintype belongs to one
         and only one (kinterm) category (producing what I call a neat partition of the set of
         kintypes) and each (kinterm) category contains more than zero but less than nine
         kintypes. It follows that a categorization may contain anywhere from one to eight
         categories.
            They use an assumption derived from the work of Bruner, Goodnow, and Austin
         (1956) to eliminate the great majority of these logically possible categorizations (Nerlove
         and Romney 1967:181). Bruner, Goodnow, and Austin (1956:41-43) found that
         conjunctively defined categories (characterized by the joint presence of the appropriate
         values of one or more attributes) are much more easily learned than are disjunctively
         defined categories (characterized by the individual presence of the appropriate value of at
         least one of two or more attributes). A category that includes all and only big blue balls is
         conjunctively defined, while a category that includes all and only balls that are blue
         and/or big is disjunctively defined. Nerlove and Romney also make some use of Bruner,
         Goodnow, and Austin's related finding that conjunctively defined categories based on
         absolute attributes (i.e., where the presence of the appropriate value of the attribute can
         be determined from the examination of a single object) are somewhat easier to learn than
         are ones based on relative attributes (i.e., where the presence of the appropriate value of
         the attribute can only be determined from the comparison of two objects). Color is an
         absolute attribute; one can tell by a direct examination of a given ball whether or not it is
         blue. Size is a relative attribute; one needs to compare a given ball with another (or some
         other equivalent standard) to determine whether the indicated ball is big or small.5

   In order to apply Bruner, Goodnow, and Austin's findings to the classification of
siblings, Nerlove and Romney "propose to view sibling terminology as the outcome of a
(natural' cognitive experiment, and on this basis assume that disjunctive categories would
be extremely rare. This assumes that an 'easier' system has a higher survival value than a
'difficult' system" (1967:181). However, one still cannot classify kinterms as con-
junctively or disjunctively defined until one knows what attributes are used to define
these kinterms. Presumably as a result of an examination of empirical cases, they
determine that there are four semantic features that can function as attributes of sibling
terminological categories. Three of these are the variables which they used to define the
set of kintypes: sex-of-speaker, sex-of-relative, and relative-age. A fourth, relative-sex
(sometimes symbolized as x/ / below), is defined by a comparison of sex-of-speaker with
sex-of-relative; this feature has the value "same" (symbolized by / /) if sex-of-speaker is
the same as sex-of-relative and "opposite" (symbolized by x) if sex-of-speaker and
sex-of-relative are absolute attributes while the features relative-age and relative-sex are
relative attributes. As they state, each of these semantic features can be utilized in a given
terminology as a primary component or distinction (which divides the complete set of
eight true sibling kintypes into subsets of four kintypes each), as a secondary component
or distinction (which divides a subset containing four of the kintypes into two subsets,
each of which contains two kintypes), or as a tertiary component or distinction (which
divides a subset containing two of the kintypes into two subsets, each of which contains a
single kintype) (1967:183).
   Eliminating all categorizations that contain any disjunctively defined categories
eliminates 3,698 of the 4,140 logically possible types of categorizations. These eliminated
categorizations include ones such as (mome, fome, mofe, fofy) (momy, fomy, mofy,
fofe) and (momy, fofe) (mome, fomy, mofy) (fofy, fome, mofe), in which none of the
categories can be conjunctively defined, as well as ones such as (mome, fofe) (momy)
(fome, mofe) (fofy, fomy, mofy), in which some of the categories can be conjunctively
defined (as, 'elder same sex sibling,' 'younger same sex male sibling,' and 'elder opposite
sex sibling,' respectively), but in which at least one of the categories cannot be
conjunctively defined (as [fofy, fomy, mofy], the category into which falls 'either a
younger same sex female sibling or a younger opposite sex sibling'-'younger sibling' will
not work as a definition since momy is in another category).
   Nerlove and Romney use five additional assumptions to eliminate the remaining
empirically non-occurrent categorizations: (1) Two relative components will not occur as
primary; that is, relative age and same-sex/opposite-sex distinctions will not occur
together as primary. (2) If sex-of-relative is secondary to relative-age, it will occur in the
elder rather than the younger category. (3) If relative-age occurs as a secondary
distinction with sex-of-relative primary, it will be on the male side. (4) If relative-age
occurs as a secondary distinction, with same-sex/opposite-sex primary, it will be on the
same-sex side. (5) Sex-of-speaker may appear as a tertiary distinction (only) (Nerlove and
Romney 1967:183).
   They seem to have made an additional implicit assumption to the effect that relatively
difficult attributes (i.e., semantic features) will be used less completely than will relatively
easy ones, i.e., will pertain to fewer categories in a categorization. Such a notion seems
necessitated by their explanations of assumptions (1) and (5). Their first numbered
assumption is derived from Bruner, Goodnow, and Austin's finding that conjunctively
defined categories based on relative attributes are somewhat harder to learn than are ones
based on absolute attributes. Nerlove and Romney appear to feel that a categorization

         be great enough, it would also preclude it from being a secondary distinction-i.e., would

         from Greenberg's universal and say that "sex-of-speaker distinctions are only made with
         reference to relatives in lower generations and thus will not occur as primary distinctions
         in sibling terminologies" (1967:182). If "only" excludes the speaker's own generation,
         then their assumption (5) would be invalid, and so I infer that their extrapolation means
         that sex-of-ego can only be a feature of younger relatives in speakers own generation and
         thus that sex-of-ego cannot be a primary distinction. However, this line of reasoning does
         not explain why sex-of-ego should not be a secondary distinction.

   Their assumption 5 (sex-of-speaker may appear as a tertiary distinction) is based on
Greenberg's (1966:78-79) discussion of sex-of-ego as a kinterm feature. Greenberg is
explicitly talking about generations other than GO; he says that sex-of-speaker occurs as a
feature of descending generation terms that are true-reciprocals of ascending terms based
on sex-of-relative. (Psychologically, the distinguishing feature of the descending term, x,
seems to be "reciprocal-of-x" rather than "sex-of-speaker.") The only exact analog in GO
would be the case of relative-age as a primary feature, where older siblings were
distinguished by sex-of-relative (as a secondary feature) and younger siblings, reciprocally,
by sex-of-speaker (as a secondary feature), a case which does not occur in Nerlove and
Romney's data.
   Greenberg's findings seem to depend on a situation in which the senior relative has his
social position clearly established before the junior enters on the scene; his position has to
be different from the one the junior initially assumes upon appearing, but it also has to be
one in which the junior has a long-term interest. The kind of relationship being discussed
is focused particularly on the senior relative who has the position, rather than being
focused on the relationship between the two relatives, as are most other terms. For
example, with the Fanti, a+fom (wofa) is the person from whom I inherit; when I am
born, he already has property and position in the lineage. On the other hand, mof-a
(awofasi) has nothing and is simply the person who inherits from me. The Fanti seem
more concerned with the role of the wofa than with that of the awofasi. Older siblings are
not usually as clearly established at the birth of younger siblings as are relatives of
ascendent generations; therefore, it seems unlikely to me that Greenberg's findings could
be used as the basis for inferring that sex-of-speaker is ever used as a distinctive feature of
sibling terminologies.
   Having seen a general reason for not expecting sex-of-speaker ever to be a distinctive
feature of sibling terminologies, let us next examine what use Nerlove and Romney make
of this feature in their sibling terminology typology. Their reading of the implications of
Greenberg's findings for sibling terminologies excludes all uses of sex-of-speaker except
those in which younger sibling terms generated by sex-of-speaker are true reciprocals of
elder sibling terms generated by sex-of-relative. Any use of sex-of-speaker as a primary
distinction is obviously ruled out. The only possible type in which sex-of-speaker could
be a secondary distinction is the one just discussed in which relative age is the primary
distinction, sex-of-relative is the secondary distinction on the elder side, and sex-of-
speaker (reciprocally) is the secondary distinction on the younger side.

   Thus, their assumption 5 should be replaced with an assumption that sex-of-speaker
simply will not be a feature of sibling terms. I think that a consideration of cognitive
economy indicates some further reasons for ruling out sex-of-speaker as a feature in
anything but the true-reciprocal or self-reciprocal situation.
   Bruner et al. suggest that we handle cognitive problems best where we can have simple
if-and-only-if definitions of categories in terms of single sets of features. Conjunctive
categories seem to be preferred over relative categories because the former consist wholly
of absolute features that require only a single observation for evaluation, while the latter
necessitate more work, requiring a comparison of two observations for categorization.
This is the basis for Nerlove and Romney's assumption 1. The actual observations that a
child would have to make to form the sex-of-speaker concept (other than in the
reciprocal situation discussed above) would seem to be mildly complex, involving the
conception of oneself as a classifiable concept in the world. This "decentering" (or the
realization that the world does not exist only from the one perspective of one's own self)
is something that Piaget and others think a child has trouble doing at the age at which he
would be learning sibling terminological categories (cf. Flavell 1963:230-231). This
argument about cognitive complexity completes the reasoning behind my version of
assumption 5. How a child perceives his kinsmen and learns his kinship categorizations is
a relevant consideration for this argument: one presumes that it is through the process of
transmission from generation to generation that the individual's tendency to infer regular
conjunctive attributes from the set of instances to which he is exposed comes to affect an
interpersonal structure such as language (also here cf. Brown 1965:247)-Nerlove and
Romney's " 'natural' cognitive experiment."
   Having finished revising Nerlove and Romney's assumptions, we can now proceed to
my two additional assumptions. We saw above that there are a number of types (my Type
I situation) based on the features, sex-of-elder-sibling and sex-of-younger-sibling, which
are not among Nerlove and Romney's twelve empirically occurrent types, but which are
not eliminated by their explicit assumptions. These two features would be even more
complex than sex-of-speaker since they first require the comparison of two observations
(as does a relative feature), but then also require a subsequent observation, not of an
absolute thing, but of the result of the first comparison. It does get hard to keep straight;
Bruner et al.'s work on concept formation strategies (1 956:Chapter 4) shows that people
prefer strategies that are relatively easy to keep straight. On this basis we can add a new
assumption: (6) sex-of-elder-sibling and sex-of-younger-sibling will not occur as features
of sibling terms.
   Let us now turn to the situation represented by the use of x/ / (relative-sex) as a
secondary (or tertiary?) distinction. Nerlove and Romney propose no such type in their
list of twelve types and indicate that no such types occur empirically. However, they
offer no assumption that would rule out such types. I suggest, as a new assumption (7),
that relative-sex has logical properties involving self-reciprocality that prevent it from
being used as a secondary distinction or, in all but one special case, as a tertiary
distinction. The special case is the separation of mome from fome in Nerlove and
Romney's Types 3, 5, and 6.

   If one assumes, with me, that distinctions get coded in a terminology for specific
reasons (even if we do not yet have a good theory about these reasons), then one can ask
what happens when the reasons seemingly operate so as to imply a disjunctive
terminology. In particular, there are two things that can happen: (1 ) the partition of
sibling set that Nerlove and Romney assume (in which each sibling type belongs to one
and only one sibling term) can break down, or (2) the conjunctivity assumption can break
down. The Fanti apparently have such pressures toward disjunctivity: for most purposes,
siblings are sufficiently similar not be terminologically distinguished, but mof must be
distinguished because she is a man's terminological link to his matrilineal heirs (his

neatly partitioned, solution, which is the one I would have predicted prior to reading
Nerlove and Romney's article and prior to seeing the Fanti, would be for them to have
terms: (1) mof and (2) mom, fom, fof. The conjunctive, non-neat-partition, solution that
the Fanti in fact have chosen also requires two terms: (1) mof and (2) aoa (i.e., mom,
fom, fof, and mof). The Fanti clearly are preserving conjunctivity-even at the price of
having overlapping terms. The Fanti case, in turn, makes me curious about the five
disjunctive cases reported by Nerlove and Romney; could, perhaps, the ethnographers
involved have missed similar overlapping kinterms; will assumption (1) always break down
before (2) or are there genuine disjunctive categories in which (2) breaks down first? Faris
(1969) has suggested that an apparently disjunctive (sixth) case is really a polysemous
case involving sex-of-ego as a feature. I suggest, on the basis of the material he presents,
that his Nuba case is essentially the same as my Fanti one: a male's sister is that male's
terminological connection to his matrilineage descendents and is specially labelled as
such, but in other contexts is simply a sibling and labeled by the general sibling term.
Faris's statement that "mixed siblings are lay," the general sibling term (1969:483),
provides some support for this view. But whetherV I am right or wrong about Nuba, both
Faris's observations about that case and mine about Fanti indicate the need for additional
ethnographic study, in view of Nerlove and Romney's findings, of the five apparently
disjunctive cases that they found in the literature. The alternatives that Faris and I suggest
have both not normally been considered by fieldworkers studying kinship systems until
recently.
