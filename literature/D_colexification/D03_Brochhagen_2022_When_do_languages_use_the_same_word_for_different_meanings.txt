When do languages use the same word for different
          meanings? The Goldilocks Principle in
                               colexification


            This manuscript was compiled on April 13, 2022


                                    Abstract

          Lexical ambiguity is pervasive in language, and often systematic.
      For instance, the Spanish word dedo can refer to a toe or a finger, that
      is, these two meanings colexify in Spanish; and they do so as well in
      over one hundred other languages. Previous work shows that related
      meanings are more likely to colexify. This is attributed to cognitive
      pressure towards simplicity in language, as it makes lexicons easier to
      learn and use. The present study examines the interplay between this
      pressure and the competing pressure for languages to support accu-
      rate information transfer. We hypothesize that colexification follows
      a Goldilocks principle that balances the two pressures: meanings are
      more likely to attach to the same word when they are related to an
      optimal degree —neither too much, nor too little. We find support
      for this principle in data from over 1200 languages and 1400 mean-
      ings. Our results thus suggest that universal forces shape the lexicons
      of natural languages. More broadly, they contribute to the growing
      body of evidence suggesting that languages evolve to strike a balance
      between competing functional and cognitive pressures.

Keywords: language universals; colexification; cognitive effort; ambiguity; effi-
cient communication

 1   1        Introduction
 2   The association of multiple meanings with the same form is pervasive across
 3   natural languages (Murphy 2002, Wasow et al. 2005, Wasow 2015, Dautriche
 4   2015), a phenomenon called colexification (François 2008). For instance,
 5   as illustrated in Figure 1A, the Spanish word dedo can refer to both a finger
 6   and a toe; that is, unlike English, Spanish colexifies these two meanings,
 7   using a single word to express both.1 Many colexifications are attested
 8   throughout the world (François 2008, Srinivasan and Rabagliati 2015, Youn
 9   et al. 2016, Jackson et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2020a). For instance, the conflation
10   of toe and finger is found in at least 135 languages (Rzymski et al. 2020),
11   many of which are phylogenetically unrelated and spoken in different parts
12   of the globe. This suggests that universal forces are at play, giving rise to
13   systematic cross-linguistic patterns.
14           This study investigates how the interplay between two major forces
15   shapes the lexical structure of natural languages, using large-scale cross-
16   linguistic data about colexification. The first force is cognitive pressure for
17   simplicity. A number of studies suggest that aspects of languages that are
18   easier to learn and use will tend to be favored over time (a.o., Kirby and
19   Hurford 2002, Smith et al. 2003, Kirby et al. 2014). Regarding the lexicon,
20   in the extreme, a very simple language could colexify all meanings, using a
21   single word form to express them all. However, while very easy to learn and
22   store, this language would not be very useful from a communicative point of
23   view. Indeed, a competing force drives languages to complexity: the need
24   for them to be informative, in the sense of supporting accurate informa-
25   tion transfer (a.o., Zipf 1949, Martinet 1962, Horn 1984, Jäger and van Rooij
26   2007, Piantadosi 2014, Christiansen and Chater 2008, Regier et al. 2015).
27   At the other extreme, then, a maximally informative lexicon could have one
28   distinct word per meaning, with no ambiguity. However, this would create
29   larger lexicons that would be more difficult to learn and use: new mean-

36   ity (Christiansen and Chater 2008, Regier et al. 2015, Kirby et al. 2015,
37   Brochhagen et al. 2018, Gibson et al. 2019, Carr et al. 2020, Mollica et al.

40   These include color (Zaslavsky et al. 2018); numerals (Xu et al. 2020b);
41   quantifiers (Steinert-Threlkeld 2021); containers (Xu et al. 2016); indefinites
42   (Denić et al. 2021); and kinship (Kemp and Regier 2012). In the present
43   study, we examine the interaction between simplicity and informativeness in
44   the lexicon at a broader scale, covering over 1400 meanings and more than
45   1200 languages.
46      We build on recent work that suggests that related meanings, like fin-
47   ger and toe, tend to be expressed by the same word more than unrelated
48   meanings (Xu et al. 2020a, Karjus et al. 2021). This tendency has been
49   attributed to cognitive pressure for simplicity. The structure of lexicons as
50   well as semantic memory may favor the colexification of meanings that are
51   easy to relate to one another. This has been argued to assist vocabulary
52   acquisition (with established word-meaning associations providing a scaffold
53   for new meanings), as well as lexical retrieval and interpretation (Srinivasan
54   and Rabagliati 2015, Ramiro et al. 2018, Xu et al. 2020a). However, in
55   line with Karjus et al.’s (2021) findings using artificial languages, we hy-
56   pothesize that informativeness may counterbalance the tendency to colexify
57   related meanings: If meanings are too related, then expressing them with
58   the same form can be disadvantageous from a functional, communicative
59   point of view. For instance, left and right are highly related but are
60   often relevant alternatives in context. Consider someone giving directions;
61   if they say go left, there is often the contextually relevant alternative of go-
62   ing right. Thus, using the same form for left and right risks leading to

63   communicative failure. Indeed, the possibility to contextually disambiguate
64   meanings is crucial for the persistence of lexical ambiguity (Piantadosi et al.
65   2012, Santana 2014, Brochhagen 2020).
66      Note that it is always possible to disambiguate meanings using longer
67   expressions; for instance, Spanish speakers can use dedo del pie (‘finger of
68   the foot’) when they need to unambiguously refer to a toe. Analogously, it
69   would also be possible to use a single word, for instance dax, for left and
70   right, and to use a more complex expression to distinguish between the two.
71   What is at stake is thus not whether languages can express a given semantic
72   distinction, but whether they care enough about it to encode it in the lexicon.
73   The prediction is that, on average, they will care more about distinctions
74   that are often alternatives in context, because the communicative need to
75   distinguish them is higher, with context providing less information to tease
76   them apart.
77      To sum up, we expect the communicative need to distinguish meanings to
78   play a role in shaping lexicons across languages. Communicative need varies
79   across language communities depending on factors such as environment and
80   culture (Kemp et al. 2018, Jackson et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2020a). However, we
81   predict that the pressure for informativeness will show a universal signature
82   over and above such language-specific variation.
83      More concretely, we hypothesize that colexification follows a Goldilocks
84   principle: meanings colexify if they are neither too unrelated, nor too re-
85   lated, but, as in the fairy tale Goldilocks and the Three Bears, “just right”.
86   The Goldilocks principle is illustrated in Figure 1B. Crucially, following the
87   hypothesis that what hinders communication is meaning confusability in
88   context (Piantadosi et al. 2012, Brochhagen 2020), we expect “too related”
89   to mean “too confusable”. In other words, we expect colexification likeli-
90   hood to decrease for highly related meanings where confusability is at stake.
91   As discussed above, this particularly concerns sets of meanings that are con-
92   trasting alternatives to each other. Examples of such meanings are weekdays
93   such as monday and tuesday, meanings related to quantification like some
94   and all, and opposites like warm and cold.
95      We find support for the Goldilocks principle in two analyses. The first

96    uses data-induced measures of semantic relatedness to characterize how
97    likely meanings are to colexify. As hypothesized, we find that colexifica-
98    tion likelihood increases with semantic relatedness, until an inflection point
99    is reached for highly related meanings. However, a decrease in likelihood is
100   only partially confirmed: while the data is best characterized by a decrease,
101   it is also consistent with a plateu, suggesting that informativeness may ex-
102   ert less force than we expected a priori. The second analysis further probes
103   the role of confusability in the shift in colexification likelihood for related
104   meanings. We find that meanings that are often alternatives in context, in
105   particular those that express opposites, are indeed less likely to colexify than
106   other kinds of related meanings. Our results thus support the hypothesis
107   that natural language lexicons evolve to strike a balance between competing
108   pressures for simplicity and informativeness.

131   2.1       Models
132   We use generalized additive logistic models (Wood 2017), which allow for
133   non-linear relationships between the dependent variable and independent
134   variables. This makes them suitable to probe our hypothesized relationship
135   between colexification likelihood and semantic relatedness (Figure 1B). Gen-
136   eralized additive models include a penalization against excessive curvature:
137   “wigglier” trends, such as the Goldilocks curve compared to a (more) linear
138   relationship, are only established if they substantially improve the model
139   fit.4

183   2.2   Estimating semantic relatedness
184   We follow previous work in using words as surrogates for meanings when
185   estimating semantic relatedness (e.g., Xu et al. 2020a, Karjus et al. 2021,
186   Westera et al. 2021). More specifically, we use words in Dutch and English
187   (previous work used English only). As illustrated in Figure 1C, the related-
188   ness of word pairs, such as teen-vinger in Dutch or the equivalent toe–finger
189   in English, are used as an estimate for the relatedness of their meanings
190   (toe-finger). These estimates are then used to predict the colexification
191   likelihood of meanings in other languages (Eq. 1). It would be desirable to
192   use more –and more linguistically diverse– languages to estimate semantic
193   relatedness; however, at present only Dutch and English have resources that
194   are large enough, and of a high enough quality, for our analysis. SI Section
195   1 discusses this issue in more detail.
196      Building on Xu et al. (2020a), we evaluate three measures of semantic

198   component of these two measures.6
199       Distributional similarity measures how similar the contexts of use of dif-
200   ferent linguistic expressions are, quantifying their contextual overlap based
201   on large amounts of data, typically text corpora (Harris 1954, Lund and
202   Burgess 1996, Landauer and Dumais 1997). The Dutch and English dis-
203   tributional models that we use are from fastText (Grave et al. 2018). To
204   illustrate the measure, the contexts of use of left and right are quite simi-
205   lar (distributional similarity of 0.57 in the English model, with 1 being the
206   maximum); toe and finger are also quite similar but less so (0.47); and toe
207   and bird are, expectedly, the least similar of these pairs (0.06).
208       Associativity is derived from large-scale association norms from De Deyne
209   et al. (2013; 2018), obtained by asking subjects to produce words in response
210   to a cue. For instance, when prompted by the word toe, a given subject may
211   produce foot, finger, or nail. Following De Deyne et al. (2016; 2018), we
212   consider three different transformations of the raw cue-response counts as
213   measures of associativity. The measures are laid out in SI Section 1.3. In
214   the main text, we report results for the best one. Model comparison by
215   means of differences in expected log point-wise predictive densities indicates
216   that this is the most sophisticated, random-walk based, transformation (see
217   SI Table S3). This is consistent with De Deyne et al.’s (2018) evaluation
218   of these transformations on other semantic tasks. Using the examples from
219   above and the English associativity scores that we use in this study, left and
220   right have an associativity of 0.42 (maximum is 1); toe and finger score 0.41;
221   and toe and bird score 0.02.
222       Distributional similarity and associativity codify different facets of se-
223   mantic relatedness, but they do not strongly diverge either. They have a
224   Pearson’s correlation of 0.7 for Dutch resources; 0.82 for English resources;
225   and 0.76 overall. To intuitively exemplify where they may differ: car is dis-

276   3    Confusability decreases colexification likelihood
277   The results so far suggest that there is a shift in colexification likelihood for
278   highly related meanings; however, our hypothesis specifically predicts that
279   the shift is due to confusability, rather than high semantic relatedness per
280   se. We next probe the role of confusability directly.
281       As discussed above, we expect communicative pressure to make it less
282   likely for languages to colexify meanings that often express contrasting al-
283   ternatives to each other in context. In Figure 2B, this is exemplified by
284   the pairs north-south, stallion-mare and thursday-thursday. The
285   notion of contextually relevant alternative is intuitively clear and relevant
286   to many areas of linguistics, but to the best of our knowledge no indepen-
287   dent definition of it exists (see Buccola et al. 2021 for further discussion).
288   For this reason, we focus on opposites (e.g., left and right), a subset of
289   such contextually relevant alternatives for which independent resources exist
290   (Fellbaum 2015).
291       Opposite meanings express contrasts, being maximally similar in every
292   respect but one (Tversky 1977, Chiarello et al. 1990, Mohammad et al. 2013,
293   Kliegr and Zamazal 2018). Therefore, losing the semantic distinction that
294   they encode can be expected to be particularly harmful in communicative
295   terms. Intuitions along these lines have been put forward in past stud-
296   ies (François 2008, Xu et al. 2020a); we here make a specific prediction,
297   grounded in broader theoretical considerations, and probe it empirically.
298   As comparison points, we choose two semantic relations that do not nec-
299   essarily lead to high confusability and can also be estimated from existing
300   resources (Fellbaum 2015): part-whole (e.g., toe-foot) and subsumption
301   (e.g., calf-cattle; calves are cattle, therefore cattle subsumes calf).

                                             13


===== PDF PAGE 0015 / 0029 | Native =====
302   Note that colexifying meanings connected by these relationships also im-
303   plies losing a potentially useful semantic distinction. However, we expect
304   their rate of colexification to be higher than that of opposites, under the
305   assumption that functional pressure exerts less force to lexically distinguish
306   them.
307      For this analysis, colexification rates for the different semantic rela-
308   tions were estimated from 1416 meanings and 2279 languages from CLICS3
309   (Rzymski et al. 2020). Semantic relations were extracted from WordNet
310   (Fellbaum 2015), a human-annotated lexical database, using English words
311   as proxies for meanings. The primary WordNet unit is the so-called synset,
312   or set of synonyms, aimed at representing a given sense of a word. A word
313   can be included in different synsets. In this analysis, each meaning was rep-
314   resented by the most frequent synset of its English lexification in CLICS3 .
315   The following semantic relations between synset pairs were then retrieved:
316   antonymy (for opposite meanings), holonymy and meronymy (part-whole),
317   and hyponymy and hypernymy (subsumption). The obtained data cor-
318   respond to 79 antonyms, 70 holo-/meronyms, 155 hyper-/hyponyms, and
319   1,001,438 pairs that stand in none of these three relations. Data not cov-
320   ered by WordNet was not included in the analysis. Further details and
321   descriptive statistics are given in SI Section 1.5.

324   ships. These results suggest, first, that standing in one of the three semantic
325   relations increases the odds for meanings to colexify compared to the control
326   group ‘none/other’; and second, that not all relations are equally conducive
327   to colexification. In particular, as predicted, meanings that stand in oppo-
328   sition to one another are less likely to be expressed by the same form than
329   those standing in part-whole or subsumption relations. As in the preceding
330   analysis, thus, we find that semantic relatedness renders colexification more
331   likely; and, moreover, we show that the need to distinguish meanings that
332   are particularly confusable can counteract this trend. In our interpretation,

344   4        Discussion
345   We have found empirical support for a Goldilocks principle in colexification:
346   Meanings are more likely to be expressed by the same word when they are
347   neither too unrelated, nor too related, but just right. This pattern is pre-
348   dicted by the measure of semantic relatedness that best characterizes the
349   colexification data: a synthesis of distributional similarity and associativity.
350   More specifically, our results suggest that the Goldilocks zone of colexifica-
351   tion is composed of meanings that are related enough that colexifying them
352   fosters cognitive economy (Xu et al. 2020a, Karjus et al. 2021), and at the
353   same time are not too confusable in actual language use (Karjus et al. 2021).
354   Our interpretation is that natural language lexicons follow the Goldilocks
355   principle because they evolve to strike a balance between being as simple
356   as possible while still being informative enough. That is, they do so as a
357   response to competing cognitive and communicative pressures.
358           We should stress that, while less likely according to model estimates,
359   the results of the first analysis do not rule out a weaker version of the
360   principle, one in which colexification likelihood does not decrease with high
361   semantic relatedness, but simply plateaus. This version still supports the

362   general hypothesis, namely, that pressure for informativeness counteracts
363   the increasing trend from simplicity. However, it also suggests that informa-
364   tiveness may exert less force than we expected a priori. Another caveat is
365   the fact that we have used a particular database of colexifications (Rzymski
366   et al. 2020); while this is the most complete source of colexification data
367   available to date, it could be that it leads to underestimating colexification
368   rates for particular kinds of meanings, or to other kinds of biases in the
369   results. In particular, the database covers just under 3000 concepts, most of
370   them pertaining to concrete objects that are commonly relevant in language
371   communities; and only certain subsets of these concepts are covered in each
372   of the languages included in the database. Future work should aim at an
373   even broader coverage of the conceptual domain and the world’s languages.
374      While the pattern we identified is a tendency across languages, we still
375   expect important culture-specific effects on the way languages partition
376   meanings into words, depending on their communicative needs (Jackson
377   et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2020a). For instance, while languages tend to use dif-
378   ferent words for opposites, the meanings lend and borrow are still colex-
379   ified in at least 40 languages (Rzymski et al. 2020). These languages are as
380   phylogenetically and geographically varied as Thakali (Sino-Tibetan); Komi
381   (Uralic); Guaranı́ (Tupian); and Takia (Austronesian). Also, as mentioned
382   above, using the same word for two meanings that are related but not op-
383   posites, like toe and finger, also implies losing a distinction that may be
384   relevant for communicative success. Ultimately, while one linguistic commu-
385   nity may not care to lexically distinguish lend from borrow, another may
386   not care about keeping toe and finger apart (Kemp et al. 2018, Xu et al.
387   2020a). In light of the diversity in how languages carve out reality through
388   their lexicons, it is remarkable that a signature of the universal need to keep
389   contextually confusable meanings apart can be identified.
390      Throughout this study we focused on the relationship of pairs of mean-
391   ings to better understand what drives some of them together. This contrasts
392   with the kind of characterization provided by comparative studies on seman-
393   tic maps (e.g., Haspelmath 1997, Croft 2001, Haspelmath 2003), where the
394   relations between meanings are mapped out in a network-like structure to

                                            17


===== PDF PAGE 0019 / 0029 | Native =====
395   uncover universal implicational patterns like “if a form in a language ex-
396   presses both x and y, it also expresses z” (see also François 2008, List et al.
397   2013 and Jackson et al. 2019 for network-based studies of colexification). To
398   the best of our knowledge, while being similar in scope and aims, these two
399   approaches have not yet been integrated. A promising open area of research
400   would consequently be to extend the kind of analysis conducted here to net-
401   works, and elucidate how and to which extent implicational patterns can be
402   derived from them.
403      Our findings have broader implications for phenomena regarding lexical
404   ambiguity, in particular the pervasiveness of metaphor (Lakoff and Johnson
405   1980). Previous work (Lakoff and Johnson 1980, Xu et al. 2017) indicates
406   that it is common for metaphorically related senses to belong to differ-
407   ent ontological domains, and, in particular, to vary along a concreteness-
408   abstractness axis. As an example, the verb go in English can be used in a
409   concrete physical sense (“Kids can easily go from the school to the library
410   in this village”) and in an abstract sense (“Voters can easily go from a lib-
411   eral to a conservative position in this country”). It has furthermore been
412   shown that metaphor is directional; for instance, historically, languages ex-
413   tend concrete words with abstract meanings (Xu et al. 2017). This has been
414   suggested to be cognitively advantageous, because metaphor assists us in
415   reasoning about abstract domains by extending features from domains that
416   are more directly accessible to perception (Lakoff and Johnson 1980, Xu
417   et al. 2017). Our study suggests that metaphor is also advantageous from
418   a functional perspective, because it allows speakers to conflate meanings
419   without risking communicative failure: If two meanings belong to ontologi-
420   cally different domains, then it is unlikely that colexifying them will cause
421   confusion in context. Under this interpretation, metaphor simultaneously
422   maximizes simplicity and informativeness, which would explain its vast suc-
423   cess as a linguistic mechanism. Future work should probe this hypothesis
424   directly, and further examine how metaphor aids cognition (in particular,
425   what specifically makes meanings relateable by metaphor), as well as how the
426   hypothesis may extend to related semantic phenomena, such as metonymy.
427      More generally, we contribute to the growing body of evidence that nat-

                                            18


===== PDF PAGE 0020 / 0029 | Native =====
428   ural languages are shaped by the need for efficient communication, in the
429   sense that they achieve a good balance between the two competing pressures
430   for simplicity and informativeness (Christiansen and Chater 2008, Regier
431   et al. 2015, Kirby et al. 2015, Brochhagen et al. 2018, Gibson et al. 2019,
432   Carr et al. 2020, Monaghan et al. 2011, Dingemanse et al. 2015). Going
433   beyond the restricted domains examined so far (Kemp and Regier 2012,
434   Xu et al. 2016, Zaslavsky et al. 2018, Xu et al. 2020b, Denić et al. 2021,
435   Steinert-Threlkeld 2021), our work suggests that the trade-off between sim-
436   plicity and informativeness is reflected in the way natural language lexicons
437   associate words and meanings, and how they manage ambiguity, shedding
438   further light on how universal principles shape language.
