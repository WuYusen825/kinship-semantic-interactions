               Predictive structure emerges during
            generalisation of kin terms to new referents
            Maisy Hallama , Fiona M. Jordanb , Simon Kirbya , Kenny Smitha

Abstract

   Despite crosslinguistic diversity in how kin relations map to terminology,
there are constraints on which kin may be categorised together. But what are the
constraints on kin term variation, and where do they come from? One proposed
constraint is internal co-selection — an evolutionary process where terminolog-
ical changes in one generation of kin co-occur with parallel changes in other
generations. This results in kin terms which are predictable on the basis of other
kin terms, a property we call predictive structure. To determine the strength of
this constraint, we measured the predictive structure of systems of kinship termi-
nology from 1135 languages. We found that kinship systems exhibit a significant
degree of predictive structure, and we argue that its prevalence reflects a pressure
for simplicity. We expected that this pressure would be imposed during the gen-
eralisation of known kin terms to new referents, and we tested this claim using
an artificial kin term generalisation task. Our results suggest that generalisation
of kin terms does indeed favour predictive structure, but that people’s preference
for predictive structure faces interference from other pressures to distinguish kin
by features like gender.

 2   1. Introduction

 3      When we acquire language, we learn how words map to objects or states
 4   of affairs in the world around us. In doing so, we determine what the plausi-
 5   ble semantic contrasts are in our language. Different languages reach different
 6   solutions to this problem of categorisation, as we know from cross-linguistic in-
 7   vestigations of semantic domains such as colour (Berlin & Kay, 1969; Regier et al.,
 8   2007), number (Greenberg, 1978; Y. Xu et al., 2020), spatial relations (Levinson &
 9   Meira, 2003; Majid et al., 2004; Khetarpal et al., 2009), and the focus of this paper,
10   kinship (Murdock, 1949; Greenberg, 1966, 1990; Kemp & Regier, 2012). Consider
11   the English kin term uncle, for example. An English speaker can use uncle to re-
12   fer to any of their parents’ male siblings, while a Hindi speaker has three distinct
13   terms for the same group of kin: māmā ‘mother’s brother’, tāū ‘father’s older
14   brother’, and cācā ‘father’s younger brother’. Meanwhile, in Hawaiian, the term
15   makuakane includes all of the male siblings of both parents and also one’s own
16   father. In spite of surface variation in the way kin are partitioned across differ-
17   ent languages, the extent of the possible variation is highly constrained. Some
18   theoretically possible patterns are never attested at all. There is no language,
19   for instance, with a single term for grandfathers and brothers, or for nieces and
20   grandsons. In this paper, we address the extent of these constraints on kinship
21   variation, and propose a mechanism by which they emerge and persist.




                                               2
22   1.1. Proposed constraints on variation

23      Anthropologists have sought to explain the pattern of constrained variation
24   with a small set of featural constraints such as gender distinctions, generational
25   distinctions, or relative age (seniority) distinctions (Kroeber, 1909; Fox, 1979),
26   and a more recent body of work situates these featural constraints in a hierar-
27   chical framework (Hage, 1999; Jones, 2010). Perhaps we cannot have ‘niece’ and
28   ‘grandson’ mapped to a single term simply because they differ along dimensions
29   of generation and gender? While tempting, such a solution cannot capture the
30   scope of the variation in distinctive features we see cross-linguistically. For in-
31   stance, Omaha categorises kin together from different generations with the term
32   e-nä’-hä, meaning ‘father’, ‘father’s brother’, and ‘father’s brother’s son’. In In-
33   donesian, the term kakak, meaning ‘older brother’ and ‘older sister’, categorises
34   kin of varying genders together. Furthermore, kinship systems rarely make the
35   same distinctions across the entire paradigm: in English, our grandparents, par-
36   ents, siblings, children and grandchildren are all distinguished by gender, but not
37   our cousins. And even if we were satisfied by the descriptive power of a feature-
38   based explanation, we would still lack an explanatory account of the mechanisms
39   that give rise to the variation we observe in kinship terminologies.
40      Elsewhere in the cognitive sciences, the pattern of constrained variation in
41   kinship systems has been explained using principles of efficient communication
42   (Kemp & Regier, 2012; Kemp et al., 2018). Systems of kinship terminology are
43   constrained by an optimal trade off between opposing pressures to be simple
44   and expressive; systems that fail to trade off these pressures efficiently are unat-
45   tested. Kinship terminology systems are simpler if they have fewer terms and
46   if these terms have simple, compressible meanings - disjunctive categories like


                                               3
47   ‘a sibling who is older than you or male’ are dispreferred compared to contigu-
48   ous categories like ‘sibling who is older than you and male’ (Nerlove & Romney,
49   1967). On the other hand, kinship terminology systems are more expressive if
50   the probability of correctly interpreting the intended referent of a kin term is
51   maximised. This means that expressive kinship systems have more terms that
52   each denote fewer individuals, and high frequency referents have more precise
53   terms, reducing the chance of a term being misunderstood. This account explains
54   why we have a wide range of surface variation, but also defines clear boundaries
55   on the extent of that variation: for a given level of communicative precision kin-
56   ship terminology systems are as compressible as possible, and for a given level
57   of compression they are as precise as possible. In short, we shouldn’t expect to
58   find kinship systems that are both complex and highly ambiguous.
59      Here we seek to understand the scope of diversity in kinship terminology
60   systems from an evolutionary perspective, in tandem with the mechanisms that
61   give rise to that diversity — an under-researched yet rich union between lan-
62   guage, culture and cognition (for a review, see Mitchell & Jordan, 2021). Con-
63   straints on language variation are not random. They reflect adaptations to the
64   cultural transmission of language, such that simpler structures that are more
65   learnable and expressive structures that are more useful for communicating pro-
66   liferate (Kirby et al., 2008; Kirby et al., 2015; Smith, 2022). We can reformulate
67   the trade-off between simplicity and expressivity in semantic category systems
68   as a joint adaptation to language learning and communication (Carr et al., 2020).
69   Indeed, crosslinguistic constraints on colour terminology have been associated
70   with biases imposed during cultural transmission (J. Xu et al., 2013), and cultural
71   transmission should similarly be the process by which kinship terminology is


                                             4
72   optimised for efficient communication (Kemp & Regier, 2012). Previous work
73   offers some insight on the role of learning and communication in the design of
74   kin terms: child language acquisition studies find that semantically simpler kin
75   terms like father and brother, which require understanding only a single rela-
76   tion, are learned earlier than those like uncle, which requires additionally that a
77   child understands both father and brother (Haviland & Clark, 1974). Experimen-
78   tal work finds that simpler kinship systems (where kin are categorised by shared
79   features) are more learnable by adults in the lab than those where kin terms are
80   shared by less similar individuals (Smith et al., 2020). Additionally, corpus work
81   shows that kinship terms are optimised for the communication needs of individ-
82   uals, reflecting the relative frequencies with which particular kin are referenced
83   (Anand & Regier, 2023).

84   1.2. Internal co-selection

85      What common features might we then expect systems of kinship terminol-
86   ogy to have, if they are constrained by opposing pressures to be simple and
87   expressive? One proposed structural constraint on kinship systems is internal
88   co-selection (Passmore et al., 2021): a process of kin term change where new dis-
89   tinctions or mergers in one part of the kinship system are followed by parallel
90   changes in another part of the system. Figure 1 displays an example of this pro-
91   cess. If a merger between the meanings ‘father’ and ‘uncle’ results in a single
92   term funcle for both referents, we might find ourselves in the confusing situa-
93   tion where sometimes the child of a funcle is called our brother and sometimes
94   they are called our cousin. Choosing the appropriate term becomes a difficult
95   task, since we cannot rely on our knowledge of other kin terms to arrive at the
96   correct term. We should therefore expect a second merger between brother and

101       If kinship systems evolve via linked sets of changes, then we get constraints
102   on possible relationships within our kinship system ‘for free’: if we have a single
103   term for fathers and uncles, the terms for their children cannot vary arbitrarily,
104   but also become constrained to a single term. We refer to this property as the
105   predictive structure of the kinship system. The more accurately you can pre-
106   dict terms for individuals in one generation (e.g. sibling and cousin terms) from
107   terms for individuals in another generation (e.g. terms for parents, aunts and un-
108   cles), the more predictive structure the system has. We propose that predictive


                                                         6
109   structure is one method by which kinship terminologies optimise for efficient
110   communication, as predictive structure facilitates greater simplicity. Predictive
111   kinship terminologies are more compressible, since each kin term can be defined
112   with respect to other terms (e.g. we use our understanding of the term funcle to
113   define the term brousin).
114      If internal co-selection is a driving force in the evolution of kinship terminol-
115   ogy, predictive structure should be common in systems of kinship terminology
116   cross-linguistically. However, Passmore et al. (2021) did not find consistent evi-
117   dence that kinship systems are structured predictively – at least, not in the ways
118   presumed by common organisational typologies for kinship systems (Murdock,
119   1949). In Sections 2 – 3, we extend their study using an alternative, information-
120   theoretic measure of predictive structure in order to robustly test the prediction
121   that kinship terminology systems are structured predictively. We present the
122   broadest typological analysis of the structure of kinship systems to date, find-
123   ing that the world’s kinship systems are structured more predictively than we
124   would expect by chance (if there were no evolutionary pressures imposing this
125   structure). By offering a common structural principle that underlies simplicity
126   in kinship systems, we support the claim that kinship systems are optimised for
127   efficient communication.
128      What then would be the mechanism that facilitates internal co-selection as
129   a process of kin term evolution? Why would kin terms evolve via linked sets of
130   changes? Inspired by explanations for predictive structure in other domains such
131   morphological paradigms (Ackerman & Malouf, 2013; Johnson et al., 2021), we
132   propose that pressures imposed during cultural transmission incentivise predic-
133   tive structure. Individuals faced with incomplete input when learning kin terms


                                              7
134   will predict unknown terms from their existing knowledge: the children of fun-
135   cles sharing a term seems a likely hypothesis, so upon hearing that one funcle’s
136   child is a brousin, the learner extends that term to children of all funcles, regular-
137   ising the paradigm. In light of the paucity of experimental approaches in kinship
138   studies, we offer an empirical test of whether the generalisation process presup-
139   posed by the internal co-selection hypothesis does indeed give rise to predictive
140   structure. In Section 4 we present the results of an artificial language generali-
141   sation task, showing that predictive structure emerges during generalisation of
142   known kin terms to new referents.


143   2. Study 1: How common is predictive structure?

144      If kinship terminology evolves by internal co-selection, we would expect that
145   kinship terms in one part of the system will be predictive of other parts of the
146   system. We test this claim by comparing kinship terms across two generations:
147   one’s siblings and cousins (Ego’s generation or G0) and one’s parents and their
148   siblings (Ego’s parents’ generation or G+1). Previous work has not found con-
149   sistent evidence that kinship systems exhibit predictive structure (Passmore et
150   al., 2021), but here we conduct a more robust test using a larger quantity of data
151   from a greater variety of languages, and using a richer measure of predictive
152   structure.
153      Passmore et al. (2021) measure predictive structure using a binary measure
154   inferred from the typology for kinship systems put forward by Murdock (1949),
155   which classifies kinship systems into six categories depending on the way a lan-
156   guage distributes kin terms for cousins. In their study, if the organisation of
157   kin at G+1 is classified the same as kin at G0, then that kinship system scores


                                                8
158   1 (predictive structure). If the two categories differ, the language scores 0 (no
159   predictive structure). However, this measure assumes that the ideal state for
160   kinship systems following the internal co-selection process is one of Murdock’s
161   six types, which do not necessarily exhibit predictive structure. Additionally, a
162   binary measure cannot capture the extent to which a system exhibits predictive
163   structure given other constraints, such as the number of terms or what other
164   distinctions are made.
165      Furthermore, while Murdock’s typology is still commonly used in kinship
166   research as a measure of kin term variation (Cronk et al., 2019; Trautmann &
167   Whiteley, 2012) or to infer social kin organisation (Schulz et al., 2019), it is in-
168   creasingly perceived as an inadequate and outdated description of global kinship
169   variation (Passmore & Jordan, 2020). Indeed, Passmore et al. (2021) suggest that
170   their inconclusive result reflects that the typology fails to accurately capture the
171   extent of global variation.
172      Here, we use a new information-theoretic measure for predictive structure.
173   We calculate predictive structure as the mutual information between kin terms,
174   and use Monte Carlo methods to compare each kinship system to a null baseline,
175   to measure the extent to which systems exhibit predictive structure relative to
176   the predictive structure we would expect to occur by chance in such a system.

177   2.1. Data

178      Our data on the world’s kinship systems comes from Kinbank (Passmore et
179   al., 2023), a database of kinship terminology in 1215 languages across 21 major
180   language families. Kinbank contains terms for relatives spanning five genera-
181   tions from grandchildren to grandparents.
182      For our analysis, we look only at kin terms for individuals in G0 including

                                               9
183   siblings (elder and younger) and parents’ siblings’ children, and in G+1 including
184   parents and parents’ siblings (elder and younger). Taking into account gender,
185   this leaves us with a total of 60 types of relative across both generations.
186      Limiting our analysis to two generations aligns with Passmore et al.’s (2021)
187   claim about internal co-selection, which only considers the evolutionary link
188   between G0 and G+1. Additionally, these relationships are the most reliably doc-
189   umented across the dataset, which allowed us to maximise the size of our data
190   sample. When data was missing, we calculated the predictive structure of the
191   terminology that was available, providing there was at least one term listed for
192   a person in each generation. This criterion left us with data from 1135 kinship
193   systems for our analysis.
194      In cases where more than one term was recorded for a given relative, we took
195   the first listed term for our analysis. If two or more alternative spellings were
196   recorded for a kin term, we took the first listed spelling.

197   2.2. Measuring predictive structure

198      For our analysis, we needed a robust measure of predictive structure and a
199   baseline model against which to compare natural languages. The baseline should
200   capture our expectations under the null hypothesis - i.e. that if there were no
201   evolutionary pressure for predictive structure, kinship terminologies would not
202   be structured in this manner.

203   Mutual information. We measure predictive structure in information theoretic
204   terms, as the mutual information between generations of kin terms. Between
205   two terms, mutual information tells us how much information we can obtain
206   about a term T0 from observing a term T1 . If we sum over all pairs (T0 , T1 ) with

248   high degree of predictive structure, following the prediction from Passmore et al.
249   (2021) that kin terms evolve via a linked set of changes. In terms of our measure,

251   95% of their permutations.

264   2.4. Discussion

265      In accordance with our hypothesis, we found that kinship systems have much
266   greater predictive structure than we would expect if there were no bias for predic-
267   tive structure. Compared to randomly permuted baselines, we found that natural
268   kinship systems exhibit vastly more structure; there is often not a single per-
269   mutation out of 1000 that approaches the true mutual information value. This
270   confirms quantitatively that natural language kin terms are not randomly allo-
271   cated to referents, and further suggests that systematic predictive structure is a
272   near-universal property of kinship systems.
273      We found some languages whose mutual information was not significantly
274   different from their permuted counterparts. Our analysis was very conservative,
275   and we included languages that had as few as a single term in each generation
276   recorded in Kinbank. Languages whose mutual information fell within the set of
277   values in the baseline were often those with incomplete kin term data (see Ap-
278   pendix 1), resulting in baselines with only a handful of possible permutations. We
279   expect that had the data been complete, the baselines for these languages would
280   have been more variable, and that their veridical mutual information would likely
281   be more different from chance than we see here.
282      Our results contrast with the findings from Passmore et al. (2021), who did not
283   find consistent evidence that kinship systems evolve by internal co-selection. By
284   measuring predictive structure as the mutual information between two genera-

                                              16
285   tions of kin terms, we were able to show the extent to which a particular kinship
286   system exhibits predictive structure, while Passmore et al.’s (2021) measure was
287   binary - either kin terms were predictive or they were not. By comparing our
288   results to a null distribution, we were able to measure for each language how
289   extreme the degree of predictive structure was. Additionally, we used a larger
290   quantity of data from a greater variety of languages. We are confident that the
291   results we have presented here are a stronger test of predictive structure.


292   3. Study 2: is predictive structure due to compositionality?

293      An alternative explanation for our findings in Section 2 could be that high
294   mutual information is a by-product of compositionality, the pervasive feature of
295   languages whereby the meaning of an expression is composed of the meanings of
296   parts of that expression. It is not uncommon for kin terms to be compositional:
297   morphologically transparent such that parts of their form are associated with
298   parts of their meaning. For instance, Kurdish khal ‘father’s brother’ and khaloaza
299   ‘father’s brother’s child’ share part of their form, such that khal indicates which
300   branch of the family tree these kin belong to - i.e. the father’s brother’s branch
301   (see Figure 3a). Additionally, -oaza indicates that an individual is a child, and
302   is also present in the other cousin terms pur-za and am-oaza. Labels which are
303   compositional in this way inevitably have a high degree of predictive structure.
304      Compositionality has itself been argued to be in part a product of pressures
305   for efficient communication (Kirby et al., 2015). Could the predictive structure
306   we see in Section 2 be due entirely to compositionality? If so, it would no longer
307   be clear that predictive structure emerges due to a specific pressure for kin terms
308   in G+1 to uniquely specify kin terms in G0 - instead, this could be a side effect


                                              17
309   of a more general preference for compositional structure in language.
310      Many of the kinship systems we see that have high mutual information rel-
311   ative to their baselines do not have clearly compositional kin terms - e.g. Nyoro
312   in Figure 2, and of those in Figure 5, English, Fijian, Turkish, Kankanyaey, Yami,
313   and Yugambeh have no clear compositional structure. But in order to more rig-
314   orously rule out the possibility that high mutual information is merely compo-

321   3.2. Measuring compositionality

322      As a proxy for compositional structure between parent and child kin terms,
323   we measured the normalised Levenshtein distance (Levenshtein, 1965) between
324   pairs of terms: the number of single-letter insertions, deletions, or replacements
325   needed to turn one string into another, proportional to the length of the longer
326   string. For instance, the number of changes required to change the term uncle
327   into the term cousin is 6, as none of the letters are in the same position and one
328   letter must be added. The length of the longer word cousin is 6, so the normalised
329   edit distance between these terms is 1. In other words, this is the highest possible
330   edit distance between a pair of words. To take an example from a language that
331   does exhibit compositionality, consider Kurdish pur ‘mother’s sister’ compared
332   to purza ‘mother’s sister’s daughter’ - the normalized edit distance between these


                                              18
333   terms is 0.4. The smaller the normalised edit distance, the more similar the two
334   strings, with zero indicating that the strings are identical. In a kinship system
335   that is compositional in the same way as Kurdish, with terms for offspring being
336   related to terms for their parents, the edit distance between parent and child
337   terms will tend to be low.
338       We computed the average edit distance between all pairs of parent and child
339   terms. As in Section 2, we built null baselines for each kinship system by permut-
340   ing which G0 terms were associated with which referents and measured the new
341   average edit distance across the new set of parent-child pairs generated by this
342   process, calculating z-scores for the edit distance of the true kinship system with
343   respect to this random baseline. Kinship systems were considered significantly
344   compositional if the true edit distance was smaller than 95% of its permutations,
345   i.e. a z-score less than 1.96.

346   3.3. Results

347       Figure 6 shows the average edit distance z-score for each natural language.
348   We found that languages tend not to have a significant degree of compositionality
349   with respect to their null baselines; 50% of languages are non-compositional,
350   while only 30% of languages have a significantly low average edit distance (i.e.
351   are highly compositional). Given that extreme values of predictive structure are
352   much more common (87.5%), this suggests that predictive structure is not merely
353   an artifact of compositionality.
354       Additionally, we also found that 20% of kinship systems have edit distances
355   that are significantly greater than those in the baseline, meaning that the labels
356   for corresponding pairs of G+1 and G0 kin (i.e., parents and their children) are
357   less similar to each other than they are to other kinship terms in the system.

384   3.4. Discussion
385       While predictive structure and compositionality are intrinsically related, our
386   results suggest that predictive structure is not contingent on kin terms being
387   compositional and can arise of its own accord. We also find that extremely high
388   edit distance values can correspond with extremely high mutual information val-

                                                       22
389   ues. This suggests not only that predictive structure and compositionality need
390   not co-occur, but also that they may interact to impose multiple kinds of struc-
391   ture on a kinship system simultaneously: predictive structure allows us to pre-
392   dict who is the descendant of whom, while compositionality encodes information
393   about the shared features of individuals.


394   4. Study 3: does predictive structure emerge during generalisation?

395      What mechanisms give rise to the emergence of predictive structure in kin-
396   ship terminology? Typological universals are evidence that language reaches
397   “stable engineering solutions” that satisfy the demands of the processes of lan-
398   guage learning and use by which languages are transmitted (Evans & Levinson,
399   2009). Indeed, typologically common structural properties of language such as
400   noun-dependency ordering, (Culbertson et al., 2012; Culbertson, Schouwstra,
401   & Kirby, 2020), harmonic ordering of phrasal heads (Culbertson, Franck, et al.,
402   2020), and conditioned variation more generally (Hudson Kam & Newport, 2009;
403   Smith & Wonnacott, 2010) have been shown to emerge by the imposition of cog-
404   nitive biases associated with language learning. The typological prevalence of
405   predictive structure in kinship terminology suggests a pressure for this structure
406   that we argue is also imposed during learning.
407      Where might this pressure come from? We draw a parallel with the predic-
408   tive structure of morphological paradigms (e.g. the organisation of tense or case
409   inflections within a language), which vary their complexity along two dimen-
410   sions: the number of different surface forms in the paradigm, and the predic-
411   tive structure of the paradigm – how reliably you can predict a form from other
412   cells in the paradigm (Ackerman & Malouf, 2013; Johnson et al., 2021). Acker-


                                             23
413   man and Malouf (2013) note that while variability in surface forms seems to be
414   quite unconstrained cross-linguistically, predictive structure seems to be much
415   more constrained — even morphological paradigms with a great deal of super-
416   ficial complexity are structured predictively, just as we find with paradigms of
417   kinship terminology. Experimental work has shown that predictive structure
418   licenses reliable generalisations about novel forms (Johnson, 2021); people are
419   more likely to correctly infer the form for an unknown cell if it can be predicted
420   using knowledge from another part of the paradigm.
421      Predictive structure in kinship terminology may be a consequence of this
422   same generalisation process. If learners use their existing knowledge of kin terms
423   to generalise to the terms for other kin, those forms are likely to be predictive of
424   one another. For instance, when the funcle merger occurs (Figure 1), the brousin
425   merger follows it because we take our existing information about funcles and
426   generalise that category structure to their children, inferring that they too should
427   be categorised together. The second merger takes hold because, when faced with
428   incomplete input, learners will extend the terms they have learned on the basis of
429   their existing knowledge - i.e. they will assume that the term brousin applies to all
430   children of funcles. In short, kin terms evolve to suit the kinds of generalisations
431   we are likely to make during the process of kin term acquisition.
432      To test whether predictive structure emerges during generalisation, we ran an
433   artificial language experiment where participants generalised known kin terms
434   to novel referents, taking inspiration from similar experiments where partici-
435   pants learn to generalise novel words in a hierarchical meaning space (e.g. F. Xu
436   & Tenenbaum, 2007). Participants were shown family trees with some members
437   labelled with a kin term and some left unlabelled. Their task was to assign a kin

633   4.2.3. Influence from gender distinctions in the given G0 labels
634      Our participants show a strong tendency to encode gender distinctions whether
635   they are present in the G+1 terms or not. What else could be encouraging par-

                                              38
636   ticipants to introduce gender distinctions?
637      Other than system type, the only part of the input that varied were the given
638   G0 terms. These were randomly assigned per trial, so the gender of their ref-
639   erents varied between trials. With five possible female referents and five male,
640   it was equally likely a participant saw two labelled G0 individuals of the same
641   gender or of different genders in any given trial. When they saw two labelled
642   G0 individuals of opposite genders, they may have interpreted this as evidence
643   that the kinship system distinguishes individuals by gender, encouraging them
644   to introduce gender distinctions.
645      We fit a mixed effects regression with fixed effects for whether or not par-
646   ticipants generalised predictively and whether or not participants saw gender-
647   distinct labelled G0 individuals. This analysis indicated a marginal tendency for
648   participants to make fewer predictive generalisations when they saw gender dis-
649   tinctions in G0, but the effect was not significant (b = -0.2348, SE = 0.12, p =
650   0.062) indicating that gender distinction between the G0 individuals does not
651   sway participants towards gender-based generalisation, unlike gender distinc-
652   tions between the G+1 individuals. In the general discussion we consider extra-
653   linguistic reasons why participants might prefer to encode gender.

654   4.3. Discussion

655      We found that participants are sensitive to predictive structure when gen-
656   eralising kinship terms to novel referents, and often produce kinship systems
657   with a significant degree of predictive structure. These results are consistent
658   with the typological prevalence of predictive structure in kinship systems that
659   we reported in Section 2, suggesting that this universal emerged due to a prefer-
660   ence for predictive structure imposed during the generalisation of known kinship

                                             39
661   terms to unknown referents. Predictive structure is more prevalent in the world
662   than in our experiment, but the miniature kinship systems used in our experi-
663   ment have considerably fewer degrees of freedom than natural kinship systems.
664   The fact that participants frequently generate kinship systems with the maxi-
665   mum possible mutual information is therefore extremely unlikely if there were
666   no preference for predictive structure.
667      However, participants did not always maximise predictive structure during
668   this task; they also have a preference to distinguish kin by means of gender
669   or lineage. In our exploratory analysis we found that participants’ responses
670   could vary depending on the distinctions made in their input: gender distinc-
671   tions amongst G+1 kin inspired generalisation of terms to same-gender individ-
672   uals, while gender distinctions amongst G0 kin did not have this effect. While
673   it is possible to have both gender distinctions and predictive structure, partici-
674   pants’ behaviour suggests that the bias for maximising predictive structure can
675   be outweighed in the presence of evidence for a different kind of categorisation.


676   5. General discussion

677      In this paper we set out to identify structural constraints on kin term diversity
678   and the mechanisms that give rise to those constraints, in line with claims that
679   kinship terminology systems are optimised for efficient communication (Kemp
680   & Regier, 2012). Previous work has addressed either kin term diversity or mecha-
681   nisms, but not both — by suggesting constraints that cannot explain the entirety
682   of the cross-linguistic variation we can observe and why it emerged (Fox, 1979;
683   Greenberg, 1990; Jones, 2010), or by offering mechanistic explanations based
684   on experimental data obtained using artificial kinship systems that are quite re-


                                                40
685   moved from what variation in natural language kinship systems actually looks
686   like (Smith et al., 2020). Here, we have married these two approaches. We con-
687   ducted the largest-scale typological study of kinship terminology to date and
688   have shown that systems of kinship terminology near-universally exhibit pre-
689   dictive structure. Following this, we have shown that participants in the lab
690   have a preference for predictive structure when generalising from known kin
691   terms to new referents. We suggest that the prevalence of predictive structure
692   cross-linguistically can be attributed to this preference.

693   Efficiency and kinship. Our results support the claim that systems of kinship ter-
694   minology are optimised for efficient communication (Kemp & Regier, 2012). The
695   constraint for predictive structure permits a lot of surface variation in the number
696   of terms and what distinctions they make, while constraining the space of pos-
697   sible systems to those which are predictive. In Kemp and Regier’s (2012) model,
698   simplicity is partly measured by the compressibility of kin term definitions. We
699   argue that predictive structure reflects a pressure for kinship systems to be sim-
700   ple, as kin terms are more compressible under predictive structure: to go back to
701   the example in Figure 1, we can recursively use our definition for funcle ‘father
702   and his male sibling’ to generate our definition for brousin ‘the child of a funcle’.
703   In other words, predictive structure facilitates simplicity and is orthogonal to ex-
704   pressivity - kin term meanings are simpler for being predictive, and so the cost
705   of having more expressive, granular terms is reduced.
706      Simpler, more compressible linguistic structures are associated with ease of
707   learning, while more expressive structures are associated with pressure to encode
708   distinctions (Kirby et al., 2015). We propose that predictive structure emerges
709   during generalisation: part of the language learning process where some knowl-

                                               41
710   edge of the system is available (and learned), but some innovation is still required
711   (Holtz, 2024). When the learner is faced with an inductive problem, generalising
712   from known terms to new referents who have not yet been assigned a kin term in
713   the mental lexicon, they do not innovate at random but use their existing knowl-
714   edge of the system to label the new referent in a systematic way. The internal
715   co-selection process of kin term evolution (Passmore et al., 2021) is facilitated by
716   this process of generalisation: kinship systems evolve to be more generalisable.

717   Emergence of feature distinctions. In our experiment, generalisation did not al-
718   ways yield predictive structure. Our participants often generalised other prop-
719   erties of the system, such as whether it encoded distinctions by gender or by
720   lineage. Our exploratory analysis suggests that in some cases, gender distinc-
721   tions in the input kin terms increase the chances of participants encoding gender
722   distinctions between the novel referents. Elsewhere, feature distinctions emerge
723   without a clear priming effect from the input. This suggests there may be several
724   biases influencing participants’ behaviour at this task: one that encourages pro-
725   ducing kinship systems with predictive structure and one that encourages the
726   repetition of feature distinctions. These are not by definition at odds with one
727   another: we see many participants produce kinship systems with both predic-
728   tive structure and gender distinctions. Future work could explore how feature
729   distinctions and predictive structure interact in the cross-linguistic data.
730      Innovating distinctions by gender was also common when there were no
731   gender distinctions elsewhere in the system (other than between mother and fa-
732   ther). What could encourage this behaviour? Our participants are native English
733   speakers, which could explain their willingness to encode gender distinctions
734   due to the brother/sister and aunt/uncle distinction in English. However, English

                                              42
735   does not have a gender distinction for cousins, and yet many of our participants
736   distinguished parents’ siblings children by gender.
737      There are a number of extralinguistic reasons why our participants may have
738   introduced additional distinctions not present in their input. Kin term distinc-
739   tions in the native language are deeply ingrained to the extent that they modulate
740   performance at cognitive tasks and affect what features of individuals are salient
741   (Anggoro & Gentner, 2003). The characters in our experiment are varied in ap-
742   pearance and look most similar to their immediate relatives, but gender may be
743   the most salient visual characteristic by which to distinguish individuals (at least
744   for our participants). Additionally, models of social discrimination suggest that
745   social categories like gender (and ultimately, gender bias) emerge robustly with
746   minimal pressure to do so (O’Connor, 2019), suggesting that distinguishing in-
747   dividuals by gender may be a readily available hypothesis when faced with this
748   task. Some participants also introduced a branch distinction, grouping each pair
749   of cousins together as a discrete unit, particularly for Type III systems. These
750   are indicated by visual characteristics like hair or skin colour, which may also
751   be salient visual cues for our participants. Additionally, native English speak-
752   ers are used to bilateral systems of descent, where you perceive yourself to be
753   related equally to your mother and father’s sides of the family. They are more
754   familiar with symmetry rather than lineage as a structuring element of kinship
755   terms: in English, distinctions are symmetrical on each side of the family tree.
756   This may have made our participants reluctant to introduce asymmetrical dis-
757   tinctions (where e.g. mother’s siblings and their children are distinguished at a
758   finer grain than father’s siblings and their children), even where this would be
759   the most predictive solution for Type III systems. It is possible that respondents


                                              43
760   from unilateral societies (e.g., Hindi speakers, who have additional distinctions
761   between father’s siblings compared to mother’s siblings) might respond differ-
762   ently.
763      However, culturally-motivated biases and visual cues do not form the entire
764   basis of participants’ behaviour, as predictive structure is not culturally moti-
765   vated nor visually salient, yet it frequently emerges.

766   Kinship terminology and social behaviour. In this paper we have treated kinship
767   terminology primarily as a linguistic phenomenon, and predictive structure as a
768   universal shaped entirely by biases associated with language transmission. We
769   have shown that structural properties of kinship are associated with efficient
770   communication, but the social dimension of kinship is also relevant to how we
771   communicate about kin. Therefore, it is important to point out that measures of
772   communicative efficiency for kinship do take into account that the need to refer
773   to particular kin varies cross-culturally (Kemp & Regier, 2012).
774      Within anthropology, it has long been accepted that patterns of kinship ter-
775   minology reflect patterns of social structure, and that this is a driving force in kin
776   term variation (Murdock, 1949). For instance, tolerance to cross-cousin marriage
777   may be associated with terminology that distinguishes marriageable kin from
778   non-marriageable kin (Viveiros de Castro, 1998). Recently, with the availability
779   of large cross-linguistic datasets, we see that correlations between kin term or-
780   ganisation and social practice may be confined to particular language families
781   or regions, and may not be as robust as previously thought (Passmore & Jordan,
782   2020; Passmore et al., 2023). Nonetheless, even if the link between terminology
783   and behaviour is not straightforward, future work should consider the interplay
784   between efficient communication and social practice, by taking advantage of the

                                               44
785   large linguistic and cultural datasets now available to inform cognitive scientific
786   models. Accounts of kin term organisation should be aware of how kin distinc-
787   tions are shaped by the repeated imposition of culturally-motivated communica-
788   tive need, determining how often we communicate about particular individuals,
789   who we perceive to be similar, and what distinctions we need to make.
790      That said, our experimental design offers a new paradigm for robustly testing
791   claims about kin term diversity that are removed from participants’ own cultural
792   frames of reference. Artificial language experiments in general allow us to test
793   linguistic claims in a language- and culture-neutral way. In introducing this task
794   framing, we hope to provide a new avenue for testing claims about kinship and
795   cognition, particularly in the light of new contributions to cognitive science that
796   use kinship terminology as case study to develop models of lexical acquisition
797   and semantic refinement (e.g. Mollica & Piantadosi, 2022; Markham et al., 2024).


798   6. Conclusion

799      In this paper, we presented a large-scale typological analysis of kinship termi-
800   nology, showing that the world’s languages exhibit an extreme degree of predic-
801   tive structure between generations of kin terms. We also conducted an artificial
802   language experiment, finding that participants often generalise known kin terms
803   to new referents in a way that maximises predictive structure. We suggest that
804   the cross-linguistic tendency for kinship systems to have predictive structure
805   emerges due to pressures imposed during cultural transmission: when individ-
806   uals are faced with incomplete input when learning kin terms, they will predict
807   the remaining kin terms based on their existing knowledge, resulting in highly
808   structured systems.
